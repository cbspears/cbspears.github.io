Assessment

Bid — with structure, at normalized earnings. Not a walk-away; not a chase.

  1. The build cycle is multi-year, not a spike. Hyperscaler capex is still accelerating into 2026 (~$700–725B for the big four, +60–77% y/y), 2027 consensus is now above $1 trillion, and every serious model — Goldman, McKinsey, the bears' own — carries elevated spend through at least 2028. Demand through ~2028 is contracted and financed. (High confidence)
  2. But July 2026 is at or near peak cycle tightness. Goldman explicitly dates peak data center market tightness (>95% occupancy) to late 2026 — this quarter — with moderation starting 2027 and "the risk of long-term market oversupply we see in 2027 and beyond." A seller coming to market right now, priced on a two-year revenue doubling, is selling at maximum optics. Richard's oilfield instinct is the correct one on price. (High confidence)
  3. VFDs are a short-cycle product riding a long-cycle boom. Transformers (128+ week lead times), switchgear (sold out through 2028), and gensets (Caterpillar booked through 2027) carry 2–3 years of contracted revenue that would survive a downturn. Drives do not: low-voltage VFDs are book-and-ship (weeks), medium-voltage engineered-to-order (months). If data center orders slow, this company feels it within one to two quarters. (High confidence)

Bottom line: Bid — the demand is real and the cycle has years of runway — but underwrite normalized earnings, not the doubling. Value the oilfield base as an oilfield business, haircut the data-center contribution to a sustainable run-rate, and bridge any gap to the seller's boom-priced ask with an earnout tied to data center revenue persistence into 2027–28. Framework in Section 8.

SUMMARY

Executive Summary

Richard's question — is the AI/data center build cycle short or long? — has a well-evidenced answer: long, with a dangerous middle. The physical buildout (shells, power, electrical infrastructure) is a multi-year, arguably decade-scale program constrained by electricity and equipment, not by demand. But the rate of spending growth peaks somewhere in 2026–28, the financing regime is degrading from cash to credit, and the market's own forecaster (Goldman) puts maximum tightness in late 2026 — the exact window in which this bid is due.

The evidence base: a 108-agent research sweep across hyperscaler filings, investment bank research, SemiAnalysis, BloombergNEF, McKinsey, and OEM order books, with 25 claims put through three-vote adversarial verification (24 confirmed, 1 refuted); Blockspace's internal Miner-HPC-Pivot master report (June 2026, ~54k words, claims checked against primary sources); plus targeted research on VFD market structure and valuation comps completed July 6, 2026.

What distinguishes this cycle from the oilfield booms Spears & Associates has watched for fifty years:

  • The constraint is physical and serial. Deliverable electricity — not capital, not demand — is the binding input. Gas turbines quote 3–7 years, large transformers ~128 weeks, grid interconnection a ~5-year national median. Supply cannot catch up quickly, which extends the cycle.
  • The spenders are the most creditworthy corporations on earth — through about 2028. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta fund from cash flow, though free cash flow is now falling: combined big-four FCF dropped $237B → $200B in 2025, Alphabet's long-term debt quadrupled to $46.5B, and Barclays models negative Meta FCF in 2027–28. The fragility sits in the 2028–2030 layer: AI revenue must scale from roughly $60–100B/year today toward $600B–2T to justify the spend (Sequoia, Bain).
  • The assets depreciate fast. Unlike 1999's fiber (glass that waited a decade for demand), GPUs are 3–5-year assets. Overbuild here is more dangerous than fiber overbuild — but that risk concentrates in the silicon layer. The electrical layer (substations, switchgear, generation) is 20–30-year infrastructure, and $720B (Goldman) to $1.4T (EEI) of grid spend rides behind it regardless of which AI lab wins.

For the target specifically: its two-year revenue doubling is consistent with the market, not a company-specific fluke — Danfoss's data center revenue nearly doubled in one year (to ~7% of group, ~€660M), and ABB printed triple-digit data center order growth in Q1 2026. That is reassuring about the demand's reality and sobering about its nature: the doubling is beta, not alpha. The company is riding the wave, and the wave's own cartographers date its crest to now.

1

Demand: Still Accelerating Into the Bid Window

Every hard demand indicator was still pointing up as of the Q1 2026 earnings cycle (April 29, 2026):

  • Big-four 2026 total: ~$700–725B, up 60–77% from 2025's record ~$410B. BloombergNEF's broader 14-operator universe: ~$450B (2025) → ~$750B (2026). Every Q1 2026 revision went up. [Verified 3-0, multiple sources]
  • Sell-side 2027 estimates for the 14 largest developers were revised up 56% in six months (Aug 2025 → Feb 2026, BNEF). Consensus 2027 Big Tech capex now exceeds $1 trillion. [Verified]
  • Contracted demand extends past the narrative horizon: ~$100B+ of hyperscaler–neocloud compute leases signed in the six months to March 2026, mostly 5-year terms locking demand through ~2031; Google Cloud's backlog hit $460B in Q1 2026, roughly doubling in one quarter. [Verified]
  • Physical pipeline: 23.1 GW under construction globally across 831 sites (~17 GW Americas) per BloombergNEF — at 18–36-month build times, mechanically incapable of completing as a "short spike." [Verified 2-1; end-Q3 2025 snapshot]

Read for Cowboy: the demand feeding the target's data center segment is not cresting yet on any reported number. The risk is not 2026; it is what the 2027–28 prints do to a company with a short order book.

What the Street and the Forecasters Actually Say

Goldman Sachs (the house view most cited in the deal context): global data center power demand +50% by 2027 and +165% by 2030 vs 2023 — later revised up to ~+220% — requiring ~$720B of grid investment through 2030. Their capex model runs to ~$7.6T cumulative AI infrastructure spend 2026–2031 (a model, not a forecast — the single biggest swing variable is GPU useful life: a 3-year vs 7-year replacement cycle swings cumulative depreciation between $3.99T and $2.23T). But the same shop, same analyst (James Schneider), makes the timing point that matters most for this bid: occupancy peaks above 95% in late 2026, moderates from 2027 as a supply wave lands (GS models 36.3 GW of 2027 US additions vs 13.6 GW in 2026), with explicit "risk of long-term market oversupply we see in 2027 and beyond." [Verified 3-0]

McKinsey bounds the cycle as multi-year in every scenario: ~$6.7T global data center capex needed by 2030 (band: $3.7T constrained – $7.9T accelerated); capacity demand nearly triples by 2030. Actual 2026 spend is tracking at or above their base case. Of the $5.2T AI slice, ~25% ($1.3T) goes to "energizers" — power generation, cooling, electrical infrastructure — of which ~$0.8T is electrical/mechanical equipment. Only a fraction of that $0.8T is VFD/genset-addressable — size the target's market off that slice, not the headlines. [Verified 3-0]

EPRI (Feb 2026): data centers reach 9–17% of US electricity by 2030 — a range EPRI itself doubled from its 2024 estimate. The forecasting establishment is chasing the load, not leading it. The full 2030 US demand range across BCG/BNEF/IEA/Jefferies/McKinsey/S&P/EPRI spans 206–970 TWh — a 5x spread that tells you the high scenarios assume capex compounds without a digestion phase. [Blockspace MHP Ch.10]

The bears (and this is the part Richard should weigh): Sequoia's David Cahn calculates the ecosystem needs ~$600B/yr of AI revenue against ~$50–100B actual; Bain sees an ~$800B revenue shortfall by 2030; MIT and NBER studies find most enterprise AI pilots show no measurable ROI yet; Michael Burry is short via GPU-depreciation-accounting arguments. But the bears' own models date the problem to 2028+, not 2026–27 — Barclays projects AI capex exceeding $1T and peaking in 2028. The bear case is real; it is also self-dating. [Verified 3-0]

Blockspace house view (Miner-HPC-Pivot, June 2026): "tiered credit," not bubble-or-boom. Demand through ~2028 is contracted, financed, and led by counterparties that can pay. The fragility sits in the 2028–2030 layer — lab cash burn, single-tenant neocloud credit (CoreWeave alone carries ~$20B+ of TCV on a non-investment-grade balance sheet), and the unresolved AI revenue gap. The signal to watch: the first year-over-year hyperscaler capex cut — earliest plausible sighting, the January–February 2027 earnings cycle. That's the regime change.

John's Point: Cancellations Are Real — and Mostly Don't Matter for Equipment (Yet)

John is right that proposed projects are being cancelled and delayed. The diligence question is whether those cancellations flow through to equipment demand. The verified answer, as of June 2026: no.

  • SemiAnalysis (June 18, 2026, responding directly to the "half of 2026 data centers delayed" headlines): their year-end-2026 North America hyperscaler self-build forecast moved ~1% over six months; colocation <5%. "The projects getting canceled sit in the early-stage layer that never placed equipment orders in the first place… the slot is reallocated to the next buyer in line." [Primary source, verified 3-0]
  • The OEM order books corroborate: Eaton Electrical backlog +48% y/y to $14.5B; Vertiv backlog >$15B on record orders; GE Vernova signed 21 GW of new gas-turbine agreements in Q1 2026 alone — more data center electrification orders in that one quarter than in all of 2025. Zero visible cancellation impact. [Verified]
  • The famous Microsoft "lease cancellations" of early 2025 were expired letters-of-intent and unconverted statements-of-qualification — pre-equipment-order paper. [Verified]
  • The genuine warning shots, for the record: OpenAI cut its headline compute commitment from $1.4T to ~$600B (Feb 2026), exited Stargate Norway, halted UK construction; only one of six-plus US Stargate sites is even partially energized (Abilene: ~0.3 GW live of 1.2 GW planned). The February 2026 market scare erased >$1.35T of hyperscaler market cap in a week before recovering. And Sightline Climate independently estimates 30–50% of the 2026 pipeline may slip. [Blockspace MHP; verified]

The crucial irony for a Tulsa equipment maker: most 2026 "delays" are caused by equipment shortage — switchgear sold out through 2028; Engie withdrew a 930 MW project because it couldn't procure gear; only about a third of 2026's planned 12–16 GW is actually under construction, the rest waiting on electrical equipment with 3–5-year deliveries. Delays of this kind don't destroy equipment demand — they stretch it rightward, extending the cycle for suppliers. The mechanism only breaks if hyperscaler demand rolls over broadly and the whole queue thins.

The Physical Cycle: Equipment Backlogs as the Honest Clock

Forget the narratives; the order books are the cleanest cycle-length instrument we have. As of mid-2026:

The top four rows are why "the cycle is long" is nearly a physical fact: OEMs hold 3–6 years of contracted, prepaid revenue, and new manufacturing capacity (Hitachi's $457M Virginia transformer plant, the turbine majors' 25–35%/yr expansions) doesn't arrive until 2028+.

The bottom row is why that fact does not automatically protect the target. ABB itself classifies drives as "short-cycle" business. A drives maker's data center revenue is only as durable as next quarter's order intake — unless its drives are embedded in someone else's long-cycle program.

This makes channel the single most important diligence item (Section 9, Q1). Richard said the company's VFDs "work… for running the power generation of data centers." If that means drives packaged into genset/turbine/behind-the-meter power programs — the VoltaGrid-style modular recip plants, the 29-aeroderivative Abilene-type installations, engine and turbine auxiliaries — then the target's delivery schedule inherits the genset makers' 24–30-month backlog visibility through its OEM customers. If instead its data center revenue is distributor book-and-ship into cooling and general mechanical, it has essentially no cover. These are radically different earnings-quality profiles hiding under the same revenue line.

Behind-the-meter generation, worth flagging as genuinely new demand: the "bring your own power plant" model is now the default bridge (Abilene permitted ~360 MW of simple-cycle turbines plus 1.015 GW of aeroderivatives; Meta's Hyperion runs ten on-site gas plants; xAI's Memphis campus ran ~420 MW of gas turbines — and drew a Clean Air Act lawsuit doing it). Gas recips tolerate only ~1% instantaneous load steps near full load, forcing power-electronics buffering — a structural tailwind for MV drives, PCS, and inverters that did not exist in any prior cycle.

5

The Oilfield Leg, and the Joint-Stress Problem

The other half of the target's book — artificial lift — Spears & Associates knows better than we do, so only the cycle-relevant facts:

  • US E&P discipline persists: 36 large E&Ps guided 2026 capex to $59.1B, −5% vs 2025. WTI ~$68–69 (early July 2026) against EIA forecasts that had projected ~$51 — actuals better than feared, direction still flat. Rig count 573, +26 y/y after 8 straight weekly gains, but Permian −12 y/y.
  • Secular supports are real: ESPs are ~70% of US shale lift; the ESP market is projected to grow ~7.6%/yr to 2035; field electrification pushes VFD content per well up. Artificial lift is production-weighted, not drilling-weighted — the most downturn-resilient part of oilfield spend.
  • The joint-stress scenario is the one that impairs the deal: an oil-price downturn hitting lift spending simultaneously with the Goldman-modeled 2027 data center moderation. The two end markets are uncorrelated on the way up — that's the diversification pitch — but both are capex-cycle businesses, and 2027–28 is the window where both could exhale together. Normalized through-cycle EBITDA must be stress-tested against that joint case, not each leg separately.
6

History: Which Boom Is This?

Fiber, 1999–2002 (the analogy everyone reaches for): capacity financed against circular-funded customers; when it broke, equipment suppliers (Lucent, Nortel) lost 95%+ and the equipment market took a decade to recover even though the glass eventually filled. Two differences cut in opposite directions. Unlike fiber, today's anchor demand comes from profitable, investment-grade spenders on contracted leases — better. Unlike fiber, the core asset (GPUs) depreciates in 3–5 years, so overbuild forces write-downs fast rather than waiting patiently for demand — worse. Blockspace's formulation: "the depreciating asset makes overbuild more dangerous, not less." The saving grace for an electrical supplier: the power layer is the non-depreciating part of this stack. Substations don't become obsolete when the H100 does.

The oilfield's own lesson — the one Richard already carries — is about entry price, not cycle direction. The buyers who got hurt in 2014 and 2018 weren't wrong that shale was a durable, decade-scale industry; it was and is. They were wrong to capitalize peak-year cash flows at premium multiples. Both things were true at once. That is precisely the setup here: a real, long cycle and a seller marketing peak-optics numbers into a July 2026 window that the market's own models identify as maximum tightness.

Electrical equipment supercycles run longer than either: the last major US transformer/switchgear upcycle (late 1960s–70s) ran over a decade, and today's grid spend ($1.3–1.4T planned 2026–2030 by US utilities alone) is utility-rate-base money, the slowest and stickiest capex on earth. If AI disappoints, grid electrification (reshoring, EVs, heat pumps, plain load growth) is the demand floor under electrical equipment broadly — a floor fiber never had.

Scenarios, 2026–2030

The asymmetry is the whole argument: pricing on normalized EBITDA costs you a little in the bull case and saves the deal in the bear case. Pricing on TTM hypergrowth only wins in the bull tail — and the bid is due at what Goldman calls the top of the tightness curve.

Valuation: What to Pay for a Doubling

Where the market is (July 6–7, 2026 data)

  • Public comps have re-rated 1.5–3x their own histories. Vertiv ~52x trailing EV/EBITDA (5-yr avg ~22x); Powell Industries — the closest public cousin: Houston electrical gear, oil-gas heritage, data-center re-rating — trades ~37x vs its own 10-year median of 12.4x. Eaton ~29x (pre-2020: ~12x); Schneider ~21x vs 14.7x median; Hubbell ~19x vs 16.5x. Aggregator commentary puts 20–30% multiple-compression risk on the group from a hyperscaler pause without any earnings miss.
  • Strategic M&A cluster for DC-exposed electrical equipment: ~11.5–12.7x forward EBITDA, synergized. Vertiv–Great Lakes $200M at ~11.5x expected-2026 EBITDA including synergies (SEC 8-K; trailing standalone necessarily higher); Eaton–Fibrebond $1.4B at ~12.7x; nVent–Avail $975M at ~12.5x. Outlier: Eaton–Boyd Thermal at ~22.5x (liquid-cooling scarcity asset — not this deal's comp). Where the DC halo is absent, industrial power assets clear at ~1x revenue or less (Hammond–AEG ~1.1x; Regal Rexnord's motors business sold to WEG well under 1x).
  • Lower-middle-market base rates: GF Data manufacturing ~6.1–6.5x; Capstone middle-market average ~9.8x, engineered products with IP and customer lock-in 9–12x. Capstone's HVAC-equipment read shows the measurable "data center premium" at roughly +2 turns in a year (9.0x → 10.9x); against the strategic cluster it's +4–6 turns over generic manufacturing.

Recommended framework — a sum-of-cycles bid, not a single multiple

  1. Rebuild EBITDA into three streams: (a) oilfield/artificial-lift base, (b) data center revenue at a sustainable post-2027 run-rate — we'd underwrite the doubled level held roughly flat, i.e., credit the step-change but pay nothing for continued hypergrowth — and (c) the hypergrowth increment above that run-rate, which gets valued only through contingent structure.
  2. Value stream (a) like the oilfield business it is (~5–7x through-cycle), stream (b) at engineered-industrial quality (~8–10x, reflecting real, verified demand with weak backlog cover), and stream (c) via earnout tied to data center revenue persistence through FY2027–28 — the exact period the Goldman moderation and the internal "regime change" signal say will decide the question. In practice this blends to roughly 7–9x honestly-normalized EBITDA all-in before synergies — below the 11.5–12.7x strategic cluster (those were larger, stickier, backlog-carrying assets bought by synergy-rich strategics), well below anything implied by public-comp optics.
  3. Structure beats price. An earnout on 2027–28 DC revenue converts the core uncertainty (is the doubling a level-shift or a spike?) from a pricing argument into a self-resolving contract. A seller who refuses persistence-based contingency is telling you what they think of persistence.
  4. Walk-away discipline: if competing bidders push the headline past ~10x normalized (i.e., toward the synergized strategic cluster without a strategic's synergies), let it go. On these verified numbers, the winner's-curse scenario — paying Powell-style re-rated optics for a short-cycle book at the GS-dated peak — is the single most probable way to lose money on an otherwise sound thesis.

Ten Questions for the Data Room (Before July 8)

  1. Channel and order-book composition (the decisive one): What share of data center revenue ships through genset/turbine/BTM-power OEM programs with contracted delivery schedules vs distributor book-and-ship? What is the actual backlog in weeks/months, by segment, today vs 2024?
  2. Customer concentration in the DC segment — and are any of them neoclouds or single-tenant developers (the 2028-fragile layer) rather than hyperscalers/genset OEMs?
  3. Are DC orders subject to cancellation without penalty? Any prepayments/deposits (transformer-style) or purely PO-driven?
  4. What did management's own 2027 budget assume for DC growth before the sale process began? (Ask for the pre-process board deck.)
  5. Price vs volume: how much of the doubling is unit growth vs pricing/surcharges that normalize when component shortages (memory, power semis) ease?
  6. Capacity: did they add fixed capacity/capex to serve the DC surge (operating leverage that cuts both ways), or is it flexing the same Tulsa footprint?
  7. MV vs LV mix in the DC book — MV/engineered content is stickier and closer to the BTM-generation tailwind; LV is the most exposed to a fast order-rate reset.
  8. Artificial-lift segment: ESP-driven (production-weighted, resilient) vs new-drill-driven? What did it earn through the 2015–16 and 2020 troughs?
  9. Supply chain: exposure to power-semiconductor/IGBT allocation — could their input lead times blow out and cost them share right at peak demand?
  10. Anything in the seller's timing? Founder retirement is one thing; a sophisticated sponsor selling into "the late-2026 peak" is a different signal. Who's the seller and why now?
